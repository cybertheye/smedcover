Paint the attached photograph as a very small watercolour memory fragment on a large sheet of quiet paper — what the eye remembers first, not everything in the photo.

1. Ask what the eye remembers first, and what minimum cues keep the memory of the scene. Choose exactly one unmistakable primary subject (a tightly connected group counts as one only if the photo clearly reads it as one; never a crowd) and only two to four supporting cues — a horizon, a reflection, a gesture, a colour wash, a shadow, a shoreline, a rhythm of masts, one nearby object. About three to five visual relationships in all; delete everything else.
2. Paint the primary subject first: the clearest, most recognisable, locally highest-contrast part, carrying the only restrained dark or saturated accent. Supporting cues are noticeably lighter, paler, softer, less detailed and less complete. The surrounding context is reduced to the faintest wash, partial contour or disappearing edge — never a complete environment.
3. "Fragmented" means local interruption and selective absence: partially broken contours, secondary edges disappearing into the paper, controlled gaps, unfinished pale layers — never blur, random dots, splatter, scattered filler or an unclear subject.
4. Keep the motif very small: a compact envelope about a third of the picture's width and a seventh of its height, placed near the visual centre, with even sparser actual painted coverage. Leave 82–90% of the sheet silent. When in doubt, make it smaller and remove more.
5. Transparent watercolour: diluted pigment, soft bleeding, occasional light ink, incomplete colour layers, minimal dry-brush texture, on clean warm-ivory or soft off-white paper with a barely visible cold-press texture. Two to four colour families traceable to the photo, mostly low-saturation and highly transparent.

Avoid: a full-scene watercolour, a miniature complete painting, a large motif, equal weight between elements, dense environment, heavy or opaque paint, harsh black outlines, rectangular or oval vignettes, framed panels, cartoon treatment, guide lines or annotations.
