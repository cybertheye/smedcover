Repaint the attached photograph as a refined, airy archival watercolour on lightly textured paper — a memory of the scene, not a watercolour copy of it.

1. Read the photo first: its defining subject or memory anchor; its dominant spatial rhythm, axis or mass relationship; its atmosphere, light and material; one secondary accent colour or detail.
2. Keep one or two of those structural relationships perceptible — a building's overall mass of light and shadow, the pressure of a tree canopy, a figure's gesture, the relation of a horizon. Do not reproduce every window, branch, face or object: important forms are reduced, incomplete, semi-transparent and slightly displaced, recognisable in spirit rather than in full shape.
3. Translate the rest into watercolour language: translucent washes with soft blooming edges, broken contours, partial silhouettes, a displaced echo of a shape, a few rhythmic marks of diluted ink or pigment.
4. The main motif fills roughly 42–48% of the picture; everything painted together about 60–68%; the remaining third is calm paper ground. Choose that paper tone from the photo's own atmosphere — cool mist white, warm ivory, grey-green haze, pale blue wash, snow white or tea-warm paper — not beige by habit. Let the quiet space live through faint paper fibres, diluted wash edges and gentle tonal drift.
5. Palette: three to five colours drawn from the photo, softened and lightly granulated, as pigment on cotton paper; whites are the paper itself.

Avoid: a detail-for-detail redraw, photographic areas, hard digital gradients, decorative symbols or patterns that are not in the photo, tape, frames, borders, mockups, drop shadows.
