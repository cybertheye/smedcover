Rebuild the attached photograph as the image of a 1990s pulp-print movie poster: flat saturated colour, brutal contrast, a printed sheet.

1. Colour is the hardest rule: three to four colours in total. One high-chroma background hue floods the frame — vermilion red, deep cobalt, acid yellow, emerald, hot orange or a strong hue the photo already has — perfectly flat: no gradient, clouds, texture or vignette. The rest is near-black and off-white, with at most one golden-yellow (about #F2B610) as the only other hue. Never a fifth colour for mood; separate things by value.
2. Treat the subject by the face check. Where a face must stay recognisable, keep it photographic — real features, proportions and expression, never redrawn — but push the skin to one warm tone, clothing to flat light or dark, shadows to a deep version of the background hue, and the contrast hard: highlights clip, shadows block, midtones thin out. Architecture, objects, vehicles and landscape are posterised into two or three hard tone steps with no smooth ramps; silhouettes exact, interior texture sacrificed; windows and small openings become clean geometric holes of the background colour; halftone dots or coarse screen allowed inside the steps. Simple objects without faces may become flat filled shapes. A person on Tier 1 with the place behind posterised is the strongest combination.
3. Replace the photo's background with the flat colour field; the subject's edges stay hard, never feathered.
4. Print texture is mandatory: aged paper grain over the whole sheet including the flat field, light edge wear, a soft crease, light foxing, one colour plate offset by a hair, uneven mottled ink coverage in the field (never a gradient).

Avoid: photographic realism, a film-still look, cinematic lighting, modern clean flat design, gradients, vignettes, depth-of-field blur, glow, neon, bloom, 3D, drop shadows, pastel or desaturated palettes, redrawn faces, malformed fingers.
