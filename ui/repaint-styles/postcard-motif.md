Make one sparse, hand-drawn zine-postcard picture from the attached photograph: the photo's single most attractive motif, drawn by hand on quiet paper.

1. Choose the motif with the strongest combination of distinctive identity, appealing colour, clear silhouette, strong contrast, elegant shape and relevance to the whole photo — not merely the easiest object to isolate. When the photo has a clearly dominant colour feature, the motif comes from it: a turquoise lake → its water and shoreline; a mountain sunset → the lit ridge; an ivy window → window and vines; a flower close-up → the flower, not the pot. Prefer water forms, shorelines, mountain silhouettes, plant clusters, architectural fragments, dominant clouds or shadows; avoid dull fragments and insignificant debris.
2. Draw it by hand as a restrained editorial illustration — watercolour, gouache, ink, pencil, cut paper or a painted contour, alone or combined — preserving the original silhouette, its major internal structure, its recognisable identity and its colour character, livelier than a raw crop but clean and controlled. Only where exact fidelity is essential (a face, hands, precise machinery, dense repeated detail) may a lightly softened piece of the photo be used instead, integrated into the paper.
3. Place it substantially but with space around it, a little off centre, on an even off-white or warm paper field with a faint natural texture. At most one supporting motif from the same visual story, a fifth to a third of the main motif's size, close beside it — never equal to it.

Avoid: a full-scene illustration, several equal motifs, decorative stickers or tape, heavy textures, frames, borders, photographic background.
